# Voice and Tone — The Aris Program

See also: [Messaging Framework](/program/market/messaging.md) for specific product messaging, taglines, and positioning copy.

---

## The Voice

Aris speaks as a researcher to researchers. Not as an institution, not as a startup, not
as a platform. As a peer who has sat with the same frustrations, opened the same broken
PDFs on the same phone, and decided to build something.

The register is precise, direct, and unhurried. We do not perform excitement. We do not
manufacture urgency. We make specific claims and let them land.

We are not above criticism of the status quo, but we stay concrete. "PDF fails on
mobile" is a claim. "The scholarly record is broken" is a pose. We prefer claims.

---

## Who We're Speaking To

The communities that matter most to Aris in this period:

- **Network science** — primary beachhead. Network visualizations are inherently
  interactive: force-directed graphs, clickable nodes, zoom and pan. PDF can't render
  these — it can only screenshot them. The field is also highly interdisciplinary,
  spanning sociology, physics, CS, epidemiology, and biology. A win here propagates.
  The founder works in this field.
- **Computational biology and computational physics** — secondary. Both produce the
  kinds of papers PDF actively damages: simulations, phase diagrams, interactive
  parameter exploration, figures that need to move. Comp bio watched Distill rise and
  shut down. They feel the problem viscerally.
- **MyST / Executable Books ecosystem** — already producing the HTML output that Press
  is designed to archive. The problem is not awareness of web-native documents; it's
  that there's nowhere permanent to put them.
- **Quarto / Posit community** — domain scientists using a tool, not developers. They
  are not our engineering audience.

These communities share a property: they can smell marketing copy from a distance, and
they will not forgive it. They also use AI tools heavily in their work. That is not the
problem we're building against. More on that below.

## The Central Tension: Accountability, Not Restriction

Aris's position is that every paper in the scholarly record should have a human who is
accountable for the claims in it — accountable for the methods, the conclusions, the
ethics, the errors. That person must be named, reachable, and responsible.

This is not a position against AI tools. It is a position about what a paper is.

Researchers in our communities use AI tools to write, to code, to analyze, to summarize.
That is their business. We do not comment on it. The question is not what tools someone
used to produce their work. The question is: who stands behind this paper? Who can be
questioned? Who can issue a correction? Who approved the study design?

A paper that cannot answer those questions is not science — it's content shaped like
science.

The language problem: "named authorship" sounds legalistic. "Human-only research" sounds
like a ban. Neither captures what we actually mean.

**What we mean:** There is a person responsible for this paper. They put their name on
it. If it's wrong, they can be reached.

Say it plainly. "Every paper on Press has a researcher who is responsible for it."
"Authorship means accountability." "Someone stands behind every claim." These are human
sentences. Use them.

## Register

The Aris register calibrates to context, but it never performs. Three markers that stay
constant across all registers:

**Precision over color.** We describe the actual thing. "PDF can't render interactive
figures — it replaces them with static screenshots" is better than "PDF destroys your
interactive work."

**Unhurried.** We don't ask researchers to act now. We don't suggest they'll miss out.
We put the thing in front of them and let them decide.

**Peer, not platform.** We are researchers who built something because we needed it. We
are not a company with a product for you.

## What We Say

### On accountability and authorship

- "Someone is responsible for this paper" — plain, accurate, human
- "Authorship means accountability" — compact, useful for social media
- "Every paper has a researcher who stands behind the claims" — good for about pages
- "A paper without a responsible author is content, not science" — strong; use where you
  have the room to back it up

Never: "named authorship" as the primary phrase — it sounds like a legal field. Use it
when precision requires it in grant applications; otherwise say it as a human would.

Never: "human-only," "no AI allowed," "AI-free" — these misrepresent the position and
will be read as hostile by researchers who use AI tools every day.

### On the AI era

- "An era when AI makes content proliferate and authorship erodes" — precise, not
  alarmed; good for positioning documents
- Name the specific problem: "a paper with no accountable author," "AI-generated content
  at scale that bypasses peer review"
- "Machine-mediated science" — reserve for grants and long-form prose where the reader
  has the patience budget for it; don't use in social media

Never: "AI threatens science," "the AI crisis," "AI contamination," "fighting AI." We're
not waging a war. We're building infrastructure with a clear thesis about what
authorship means.

### On format

- "Web-native" — not "modern" (modern ages poorly)
- "Legible on any screen" — specific and demonstrable
- "PDF fails mobile" — direct, accurate, verifiable
- "Format destruction" — when describing how PDF erases interactivity; don't overuse it

Never: "innovative," "cutting-edge," "next-generation," "revolutionary." These are
placeholders that researchers read as warnings.

### On Aris's position

- "We build for science on science's terms" — the organizing principle; use it as a
  north star phrase
- "Structural commitment" — not "we promise" (promises are aspirational; structure is
  operational)
- "Orthogonal, not opposed" — when drawing the distinction between science's values and
  tech industry values; this framing matters and is worth using carefully
- "The scholarly record" — not "academic publishing" (too institutional) or "the
  literature" (too narrow)
- "Open infrastructure" — not "platform" (platform implies lock-in and engagement
  mechanics)

### On accessibility

- "Legible on any screen, readable by any tool" — covers both human and machine readers
  in one phrase
- Name the standard when you invoke it: "WCAG 2.1 AA compliance," not "accessible"
- Never use "accessible to everyone" as a throwaway phrase — only when you can back it
  up technically

## What We Refuse to Say

### Growth and SaaS language

These words carry associations that researchers in our communities are trained to distrust:

- ~~users~~ → researchers, authors, readers
- ~~platform~~ → archive, infrastructure, tool (use sparingly)
- ~~leverage, unlock, supercharge, empower, disrupt~~ → describe the actual thing
- ~~seamless, frictionless~~ → say what the experience actually is
- ~~cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, innovative, next-generation~~ → meaningless; describe what makes it different and why
- ~~ecosystem~~ → acceptable occasionally, not as filler
- ~~community-driven~~ → say what the community actually does (contributes code, shapes the RFC process, runs instances)

### Urgency and dark patterns

This is a hard line, not a guideline. Researchers are time-poor and pattern-match on
dark patterns immediately.

- No countdown timers, limited spots, artificial scarcity
- No "join X thousand researchers" unless the number is meaningful and verified
- No "don't miss out," "act now," "launching soon — sign up before it's too late"
- No misleading statistics or selective comparisons

### Competitor language

- Never name competitors in attack mode
- Describe by category when contrast is necessary: "PDF-first tools," "growth-optimized
  publishing platforms"
- Exception: the aiXiv contrast is a substantive positioning distinction, not a
  competitive attack. It can be stated directly. "aiXiv accepts papers authored by AI
  agents. Press archives research where a human is responsible for the claims. These are
  different theses about what authorship means, not different implementations of the
  same thesis."

### AI positioning language

This is the language trap most likely to alienate the communities we're targeting:

- ~~"Human-only"~~ — implies restriction, will be read as hostile
- ~~"No AI allowed"~~ — misrepresents the position
- ~~"AI-powered"~~ — we don't need to boast about AI use; it's irrelevant to the thesis
- ~~"Fighting AI," "resisting AI," "protecting against AI"~~ — we are not in a war; we
  are making a claim about accountability

The correct framing: we are not interested in how a researcher produced their work. We
are interested in who stands behind it.

### Borrowed terminology with unintended baggage

- ~~"Free and open access"~~ → carries open access movement meaning (specifically about
  journal paywalls). Say: "open and free to use," "no paywall," "open infrastructure"
- ~~"Peer-reviewed"~~ for Press → Press is a preprint archive. Never imply peer review
  where there is none
- ~~"We believe"~~ → we build; belief is implied by the work
- ~~"We care about researchers"~~ → show it; don't say it

### Vague virtues

If a sentence asserts goodness without evidence, cut it or replace it with a specific
claim.

- ~~"Quality research"~~ → what makes it quality? Name the thing
- ~~"We are committed to"~~ → say what you actually do
- ~~"We prioritize"~~ → say what the priority produced

## Tone by Context

### About pages and program-level copy

Full voice. Formal-but-not-stiff register. This is where we make the argument, not where
we sell. The reader is evaluating whether Aris is worth their attention. They deserve
the full thesis, stated precisely.

Appropriate: the five-problem framework, the aiXiv distinction, the organizing
principle, "science on science's terms."

Not appropriate: bullet-pointed feature lists, urgency language, testimonials as social
proof rather than evidence.

### Social media (Bluesky, Mastodon / scholar.social)

Researcher-to-researcher. Shorter sentences. Concrete specifics over abstract claims.
Link to the actual thing, not to a landing page about the thing.

Bluesky is the primary academic social channel for our communities, especially
bioinformatics and open science. scholar.social is smaller and higher-trust — treat it
like walking into a seminar, not a launch announcement.

Good: "PDF replaces interactive figures with static screenshots. The interactivity isn't
compressed — it's gone. Press archives the HTML version where it actually works. [link
to paper]"

Not good: "Excited to announce that Press now supports [feature]! Check it out!"

A note on launches: don't announce Aris. Show Aris. A real paper working on a real phone
is worth more than a thread about what Aris does.

### Documentation

Second person throughout. Clear steps. Minimal adjectives. If something is hard, say
what makes it hard — don't paper over friction with "simply" or "just." If something is
simple, show it by making the steps short.

Never: "simply," "just," "easily" before anything that takes more than one step.

### The Hallway Track (weekly link roundup)

Leo's personal voice, not Aris institutional voice. Hallway-conversation register —
looser than about-page copy, tighter than a Bluesky thread. The value is in the
selection, not the prose. One-line framing per link: precise, opinionated, brief.

Good: "FutureHouse's literature agent outperformed PhD researchers on retrieval tasks.
The benchmark is narrow, but the direction is clear."

Not good: "Exciting developments in AI-assisted literature review this week!"

The same voice-and-tone test applies: would a computational biologist who uses GPT-4
every day read this without feeling judged? If the framing sounds breathless, alarmed,
or dismissive of AI tools, rewrite it.

### Grant applications

Formal register. The full problem framework is appropriate here. "Machine-mediated
science" is appropriate — grant readers at Sloan, NLnet, Prototype Fund have the
patience for it. Accountability and permanence claims must be technically grounded;
funders check.

Appropriate: precise technical language, five-problem framework, specific infrastructure
claims with evidence.

### READMEs and GitHub

Technical and factual. No marketing language. Describe what the code does and why that
implementation decision was made. A README is not an about page.

## The Test

Before publishing anything under the Aris name:

1. Would a researcher find this condescending? (If yes, cut it.)
2. Is this a specific claim or a vague virtue? (If vague, make it specific or cut it.)
3. Does this use urgency or dark patterns? (If yes, rewrite.)
4. Are we describing what we build, or what we believe? (Build; don't preach.)
5. Could a computational biologist who uses GPT-4 every day read this without feeling
   judged? (If no, rewrite the AI framing.)
6. Would this embarrass us on scholar.social? (If yes, rewrite.)
